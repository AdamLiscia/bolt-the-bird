- 2026-11-18 — P2, first evaluation (1,500 deck pairs, sides swapped, vs the rules-based player): 0.7%. Two bugs, both fixed test-first: (1) the cast model splits its confidence across identical cards (three Plains at 0.3–0.5 each), so a per-card threshold skipped land drops — scores are now pooled per card; (2) plans were keyed on id(game), and Python reuses ids, so from the second game in each worker the player followed a dead game's plan and passed every turn — plans now reset per game. Fixed: **23.5% ± 0.8%** (control rules vs rules 48.6%). Diagnosis: it plays lands like the rules-based player (7.3 vs 7.7 a game) but casts 6.4 spells against 10.5 and attacks 5.6 times against 8.7. People hold instants and tricks for the opponent's turn; this player never acts at instant speed, so held cards rot. Options: casting what's left in the second main phase 31.7%, attacking at a 0.35 threshold 26.9%, both **39.4% ± 0.9%**. Tuned against the same opponent, so read that as an upper bound. P2's goal (beat the rules-based player) isn't met: turn-level imitation copies people's restraint without their instant-speed follow-through. That gap is P3's job.
- 2026-11-18 — P3 designed (docs/play/P3_DESIGN.md): one network scores every legal move (state tokens for every visible object, action tokens that point at the objects they name, policy and value heads); distillation from the smart player first, then PPO self-play against a league (itself, past checkpoints, the rules-based and smart players) with a decaying KL leash, reward only for winning. Measured: ~208 decisions with a real choice per game (priority 165, attackers 25, targets 7, blockers 6); rules-based self-play ~290 games/s in one Python process; an observation ~14 µs.
- 2026-11-18 — P3a done. Bindings: the observation now carries `combat` (attackers, blocker→attacker pairs) and `legal_cards` (the card behind each object a move names, so a library search still says which card); 1 new binding test. `bolt.play_encoding` (encode: a token per visible object (own hand, both battlefields with tapped/power/toughness/damage/counters/sick/attacking/blocking, graveyards, exile, stack; the opponent's hand is a count), a game vector, and per legal move its kind, pointers to the objects it names, those cards, its numbers and the player it targets; collate pads a batch) and `bolt.play_net.PlayNet` (transformer over the game and object tokens; move tokens scored against the game token; value head in [-1, 1]); 5 tests written first: only visible cards, every pointer lands on the named object, attackers marked in combat, object order doesn't matter, a batch gives a proper distribution over each position's legal moves. Speed on the laptop CPU: encoding 17 µs a position; dim 128 × 4 layers (0.9M parameters) ~4,400 decisions/s, dim 192 × 6 (3.0M) ~2,100. About 10 self-play games a second with the network on both sides.
- 2026-11-18 — P3b done. Bindings: `Game.smart(rollouts, candidates, seed)` returns the smart player's move one decision at a time; test: with the engine's seeds it replays the engine's own smart games exactly. `bolt.play_runner`: `run_games` keeps a batch of games in lockstep with seats "net", "rules" or "smart", sends every waiting network decision through one forward pass, plays single-option moves without asking, and records steps (encoding, move, log probability, value; or the smart player's moves as teacher labels); an engine error ends one game, not the batch. `run_parallel` splits jobs over spawn worker processes and reruns a dead worker's share in a fresh pool. 6 tests written first (games finish with legal moves and full records, greedy play reproducible, rules and smart seats replay the engine's games exactly, teacher labels, parallel = serial). Laptop, 6 workers, 0.9M-parameter network: net vs net 41 games/s (5,900 decisions/s), net vs rules 95 games/s, smart 1 × 2 self-play recording both sides 14 games/s = 2,300 teacher decisions/s (160 a game), so a few million distillation examples take under an hour.
- 2026-11-18 — P3c run, three stops before it ran clean. (1) 08:51 the laptop froze and was force-restarted: the data generator (6 workers) and an evaluation smoke test (6 more) ran at once, caps 8 + 6 GB on a 16 GB machine. Rule since: one heavy job at a time, ≤4 workers, scripts/chain_p3c.sh runs data → train → eval in sequence. (2) The generator then hit its 3 GB cap (guard killed it, as designed): the main process held each shard's decisions as Python dicts; workers now compact them to arrays first (test: identical arrays), peak 1.1 GB. (3) Training on the GPU (MPS) swapped the laptop until it lagged and stalled after step 2,000: ~2.1 GB of graphics memory that guard.sh can't see, on top of other apps. A 4-minute measured test showed no leak in plain training; the stall began after the first grading pass (512-row batches of other shapes, memory not handed back). Fixes: grading in 128-row batches with the cache released after; PYTORCH_MPS_HIGH_WATERMARK_RATIO 0.3 (an error, not swap); the trainer stops itself above 3,000 MB of graphics memory; a watchdog exits after 10 minutes without a step; progress lines every 200 steps. A 650-step test through two grading passes held at 2.1 GB with no swap growth. Data: 40 shards, ~2.5M smart 1 × 2 decisions. At step 2,000 the network already picks the smart player's move 86.7% of the time on held-out games.
- 2026-11-18 — The graphics ceiling worked (step ~2,900: an out-of-memory error, not swap). Cause: ~1,400 of the 2.5M decisions come from token-filled boards (up to 462 objects and 1,579 legal moves), and one of them padded a whole 256-row batch to its size. Batches now train the ordinary rows together and each huge one (>128 objects or >256 moves) on its own; checked equal to the single padded batch (same loss, CE, MSE and agreement to 6 decimals).
- 2026-11-18 — On the GPU, the step-4,000 grading pass took the process to 4.6 GB and guard.sh killed it (as designed; the laptop was fine). macOS counts some graphics memory against the process unpredictably, so training moved to the CPU, where guard.sh sees all of it: 4 threads, ~3 steps/s, peak 1.6 GB through a grading pass, load ~5 of 10 cores. Epochs cut from 3 to 2 to keep the run near 2 hours (agreement was already 86.7% at step 2,000). Resumed from step_2000.pt.
- 2026-11-18 — P3c result (results/play_eval_distill1.json; 500 unseen deck pairs, sides swapped, greedy): the distilled network beats the rules-based player **44.6% ± 1.6%** and the smart 1 × 2 player **35.8% ± 1.5%**; the control, smart 1 × 2 vs the rules-based player on the same pairs, is 60.3% ± 1.5%. 5 of 2,000 games hit the new 20,000-move cap (a loop inside one turn). Not at the smart player's level: P3c's goal is not met. Move agreement 90.2% on held-out decisions, but 44% of decisions are passes it gets 99.8% right; on real choices: casts 83%, attacks 82%, land plays 81%, targets 73%, blocks 51%. Many misses pick another object of the same kind (another land, another attacker), some of which are the same card or the same attack in another order, so agreement understates; the losses say the rest matter. Likely causes: errors compound into positions the smart player's games never reach (the classic behavior-cloning failure), and the smart player's own choices are noisy (a 1 × 2 search with a random seed).
